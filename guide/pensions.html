Pension pillars II and III

Pillar II (kogumispension) — the awkward one

  • Voluntary since 2021. Your contribution is 2%, 4% or 6% (choosable since 2025), plus 4% redirected from social tax ✅
  • You can exit entirely at 22% ✅ — but it is all-or-nothing: you must withdraw the whole balance, which exits you from the system. Payouts happen in only three months a year (September / January / May, depending on when you file), there is a 10-year wait to rejoin, and you may do this at most twice in a lifetime
  • Unlock age = state pension age minus 5 ✅ — the "flexible retirement age". In 2026 that is 60; it rises in lockstep with the state pension age

Payout tax — 0% is achievable ✅

Payout type Tax
Lifetime annuity 0%
Fund pension over the recommended duration, paid monthly or quarterly 0%
Fund pension over a shorter duration, or paid annually 10%
Lump sum at pension age 10%
Before pension age 22% (whole balance)
Incapacity for work 0%, at any age
Paid to an heir 22%

So taking Pillar II as a properly-paced fund pension or lifetime annuity is tax-free. Only lump sums and compressed schedules attract the 10%.

⚠️ A reform is in draft, not law. A bill circulated in early 2026 would allow partial withdrawals (from ~2028) and cut the rejoin wait to 5 years (from 1 July 2027). Treat as a proposal. It would also require any future cut to the state's 4% to carry a 5-year notice period.

The FIRE tension: Pillar II money may remain locked long after an early-retirement date. But on exit, the 4% state portion stops flowing to the participant and reverts to Pillar I. Exiting therefore gives up what is effectively a 4%-of-salary match in exchange for control of the participant's own 2–6%.

That match is very hard to beat, so do not exit Pillar II for FIRE reasons. Instead treat it as your "age 65+" bucket, let it run, and size your bridge portfolio to cover from early retirement to 65. Exiting is mainly defensible if you're leaving Estonia permanently or you genuinely need the capital now.

Second-order: index-tracking Pillar II funds (LHV, Tuleva) beat active ones on fees over decades. If you're staying in, be in a low-cost index fund — check what you're actually in.

2%, 4% or 6% — should you raise your rate?

First, the fact that changes the answer: raising your own rate does not increase the state's 4%. ✅ Confirmed — Pensionikeskus states the 2-4-6 reform "concerns only the person's own contribution".

Personal rate State rate Total into Pillar II
2% (default) 4% 6%
4% 4% 8%
6% 4% 10%

Switching windows ✅: applications go through Pensionikeskus or your bank. Anything received by 30 November takes effect the following 1 January; file in December and it slips a full extra year. The last application before the deadline wins, and you can change once a year.

The 4% redirected from social tax is fixed. Going from 2% to 6% does not get you more from the state — it simply moves an extra 4% of your gross salary out of your take-home pay and into a wrapper you can't touch until 63–65. There is no additional match to capture. This is the most common misconception about the 2025 rate choice.

The tax treatment is genuinely good, though. Pillar II contributions come out of gross pay before income tax, and payouts at retirement are taxed at 10% (0% as a lifetime annuity) ⚠️ verify. That's the same pre-tax-in / low-tax-out arbitrage as Pillar III, and by the same arithmetic it beats an investment account — even if you exit early and pay 22%:

Route (€1,000 of gross salary) Invested Grows to (3×) Tax on exit Net
Pillar II, taken at retirement age €1,000 €3,000 10% €2,700
Pillar II, exited early at 22% €1,000 €3,000 22% €2,340
ETF in an investment account €780 €2,340 22% on €1,560 gain €1,997

So on tax alone, 6% wins. The reason to hesitate is liquidity, which can be decisive for early retirement:

  1. Pillar II unlocks later than everything else — 63–65, versus 60 for Pillar III. When work is intended to stop much earlier, the bridge can be the binding constraint rather than the age-65 bucket. Over-funding Pillar II can leave too little accessible capital before the unlock age.
  2. A forthcoming mortgage application. Raising the contribution rate cuts net take-home pay, which may reduce both down-payment savings and assessed borrowing capacity under the DSTI limit. Consider delaying a rate change until after the application. See property-purchase.md.
  3. Pillar III should be filled first. Same tax arbitrage, unlocks earlier, and the €6,000 allowance is per earner — in a two-earner household that is €12,000 of better-shaped capacity before Pillar II becomes the right home for a marginal euro.

Decision rule: 2% can suit a plan that still needs to build accessible bridge capital or preserve near-term borrowing capacity. A higher rate can suit a plan whose bridge is already adequately funded, particularly after available Pillar III allowance has been used. Review the choice before the annual 30 November deadline rather than tying it to a specific household timetable.

Pillar III (täiendav kogumispension) — the best-value wrapper in Estonia

Pillar III contributions can qualify for an income-tax deduction.

The deduction ceiling is not a contribution ceiling. The planner invests the full entered payment and reduces accessible savings by that payment minus the estimated refund. Its estimate uses gross salary only. Other eligible taxable income can increase the allowance; other deductions can reduce the remaining refundable tax. Employer contributions and a complete annual tax return are not modeled. Verify the final amount in your tax return. Source: EMTA contribution guidance, checked 19 September 2026; EMTA lists basic exemption and education/donation deductions before Pillar III.

Rule
Deduction limit The lower of 15% of your gross income or €6,000/year
Income needed to max it ~€40,000 gross (15% × €40,000 = €6,000)
What you get back 22% of the contribution — up to €1,320/year, per person
How Deducted from taxable income in the annual declaration; refund paid out in spring
Per person or household? Per person — but the refund is capped by that person's own income tax. A non-earning spouse contributing €6,000 gets nothing back, because there's no tax to refund. See the transfer caveat below
Separate from other deductions? Yes ✅ — TuMS §28, entirely independent of the ~€1,200 §25/§26 cap on training costs and donations
Payout before the unlock age 22% ✅
Payout at the unlock age but held <5 years 22% ✅ — the 5-year clock runs from your first contribution
Payout at the unlock age, held ≥5 years 10% ✅ lump sum or short term payout
Lifetime annuity (or term ≥ life expectancy, paid ≥quarterly) 0% ✅
Unlock age State pension age minus 5 ✅ — the same flexible age as Pillar II, currently 60 and rising. Not a fixed 60
Grandfathering The age-55 rule applies only to people who held Pillar III assets at the end of 2020
Payout to an heir 22%, as the heir's annual income
Early withdrawal clawback None — withdrawing early does not cancel refunds already received
Qualifying products Pillar III funds and voluntary pension insurance contracts

Why it beats the investment account — even if you withdraw early

The usual objection is "it's locked until 60, and I want to retire at 45". That objection is weaker than it looks, because the refund means you compound pre-tax euros.

Same net outlay of €4,680 in each case (€6,000 contributed minus the €1,320 refund), same gross return, assume the money triples over ~25 years:

Route Invested Grows to Tax on exit Net
Pillar III, withdrawn at 60+ €6,000 €18,000 10% = €1,800 €16,200
Pillar III, withdrawn early at 22% €6,000 €18,000 22% = €3,960 €14,040
ETF in an investment account €4,680 €14,040 22% on €9,360 gain = €2,059 €11,981

Even the early-withdrawal case beats the investment account — by about 17%. Taking it at 60 beats it by about 35%. The refund is doing the work: you're investing €6,000 where the IK route only has €4,680 to invest.

The one genuine cost is fees. Pillar III funds run ~0.3–0.5% against ~0.07–0.22% for a global ETF. Over 25 years that gap costs roughly 7% of the final amount — enough to narrow the advantage, nowhere near enough to reverse it. So: use a low-cost index Pillar III fund (Tuleva, LHV index), never an actively managed one.

The spousal transfer caveat for single-earner households

A transfer of unused deduction room between spouses does still exist (it survived the 2024 abolition of the separate spousal basic exemption), but with two conditions that matter here:

The Pillar III allowance is not transferable between spouses. TuMS §28²(1¹) names only §25 and §26 deductions (housing loan interest, training costs, donations). §28 — the third pillar — is never listed. This is confirmed by the statutory text, by the Chancellor of Justice's legal analysis, and by the actual field structure of Form A, which offers only housing interest, training costs and the child exemption as transferable items. Practitioner guides that describe a combined "training / donation / third-pillar" pool are simply wrong.

A transfer mechanism does exist for training costs and donations, and it requires the varaühisus (joint property) regime — but since Pillar III falls outside its scope entirely, the property regime is irrelevant to the third pillar whichever way you choose it.

The consequence for a single-earner household is now final: unused Pillar III room expires. It cannot be transferred, gifted, or applied against a spouse's tax. The only way a couple uses two allowances is for each spouse to have their own income against which to deduct. In that situation the usable capacity is €6,000 and €1,320, until the second person has taxable income of their own.

Conclusion: the allowance can be valuable for each taxpayer with enough taxable income. It does not replace the bridge fund: Pillar III is an age-60+ bucket, while an investment account can fund earlier years. Reinvesting the refund preserves the intended compounding benefit.

Should the pension count toward your FI number?

Most FIRE arithmetic sets the target at spending / SWR — enough to fund spending forever, from the day work stops. That quietly assumes no other income ever arrives, which is not true. Estonia has three unlock points:

Phase From Income Health cover
Bridge the day you stop portfolio only you buy it
Pillars II & III state pension age −5 portfolio + your own pots you still buy it
Pillar I state pension age portfolio + pots + state pension free

Note the middle row. Health insurance follows the state pension, not pension income generally: RaKS §5 insures you for receiving a state pension, and Pillar II money is not one — it is your own capital coming back to you. So the five years between the pillars unlocking and the state pension arriving are still a coverage gap, even though pension income has already started. Those five years are easy to forget and cost €272 a month each.

A plan built on the perpetual target funds the last two phases twice: the full portfolio draw and the pension. That is not caution, it is unpriced over-saving, and it is paid for in years of working. The simulator will compute it either way and show you the gap.

The case for counting Pillars II and III

They are your property. The balance sits in an account in your name, invested in funds you chose, and since 2021 the entire balance can be withdrawn early at 22%. There is no scenario short of expropriation in which the money is not yours. Treating it as if it might evaporate is not conservatism, it is just an error in the other direction.

The case against counting Pillar I

It is a promise, not an account. It is funded by whoever is working at the time, and indexed 20% to consumer prices and 80% to the growth in pension-share social tax receipts — so it tracks the size of the working population. Estonia's ratio of workers to pensioners deteriorates across the horizon of any FIRE plan written today. A government can also change the entitlement without touching anyone's balance, which is precisely what makes it different from Pillar II.

Leave it out by default. Turn it on to see what it would add, not to depend on it.

Taking the pots later than you could

Pillar II unlocks at state pension age −5. Pillar III follows the separate rules above, including grandfathering and the five-year holding period. Nothing obliges you to take either pot immediately. Leaving them invested has two effects:

  • the pot keeps compounding, so it is materially larger when it does start — five years at a 5% real return is about 28% more; and
  • the portfolio has to carry you five years longer with no income at all.

There is a third effect in your favour that the simulator does not count: an annuity bought at a later age is priced over a shorter remaining life expectancy, so it pays more per year for the same pot. Leaving that out keeps the comparison conservative.

Deferring also lines everything up — pots, state pension and free health cover all begin on the same date, which makes the plan simpler to hold in your head and removes the awkward five-year window where pension income has started but health cover still has to be bought.

Pillar II costs you Pillar I, and nobody mentions it

Joining the second pillar does not add 4% of gross on top of an unchanged state pension. It diverts it: RPKS §13¹(1) sends 16% of your gross into Pillar I instead of 20%, because four points go to your own fund. Both halves of the ühendosa fall by that same 16/20, and a member's solidaarosak is separately capped at 0.800 rather than 1.000.

The result is exactly a 20% smaller Pillar I accrual:

40 years at the average wage Pillar I
Outside Pillar II €818.32/month
Inside Pillar II €734.50/month

The flat base amount is untouched, which is why the total falls by 10% rather than 20%.

That €84 a month is the real price of the second pillar, and it is the number missing from every "the state gives you 4% free" description. It is not an argument against joining — the diverted 4%, plus your own 2–6%, plus decades of compounding in your own fund, is meant to more than repay it, and for most people it does. But the comparison is Pillar II against Pillar I, not Pillar II against nothing.

Three things that bite

Stopping work stops the accrual. This is the one most people miss. Pillar II and III contributions end the day the salary does, and Pillar I accrues per year worked — so retiring at 50 instead of 65 buys a materially smaller state pension. The simulator solves the FI date and the pension together for this reason: a later date buys a bigger pension, which in turn permits an earlier date, and the two have to settle against each other rather than be computed in sequence.

Only the lifetime annuity is lifelong. A Pillar II fund pension paced over the statistically recommended duration is taxed at 0%, but it is paced over remaining life expectancy — roughly 19 years. Start it at 60 and it stops around 80, part-way through a plan that runs to 100. Only the eluaegne pensionileping — the lifetime annuity — pays until you die, and only one insurer still writes them. An insurer may also refuse a balance too small to be worth administering. If your plan leans on pension income, it leans on that one contract being available.

The bridge is where the risk concentrates. Counting the pension means deliberately spending the portfolio down toward a smaller residual, instead of holding a pot that outgrows its withdrawals. A poor first decade cannot be recovered from: there is no income to add and no time to wait. Hold the bridge years in something less volatile than the rest of the portfolio, and keep the ability to earn something if the opening stretch goes badly.

Choosing the payout: who carries the longevity risk

The two 0%-taxed options are not the same product, and the difference decides who is exposed if you live a long time.

Lifetime annuity Fund pension
Estonian name eluaegne pensionileping fondipension
Paid by a private life insurer your own pension fund
Tax 0%, unconditionally 0% at the recommended duration
Lasts until you die about 19 years, then stops
Amount fixed in euros, for life moves with the fund's value
Buying power over time falls every year rises if the fund does, falls if it does not
Depends on one insurer still selling it nobody
Longevity risk carried by the insurer you
Inflation risk carried by you the market

Neither dominates, and the reason is the last two rows. The annuity hands longevity risk to the insurer and keeps inflation risk. The fund pension does the opposite. Which is worse depends on whether you live a long time and on what markets do - the two things nobody knows in advance.

The annuity is fixed in euros, and nothing obliges it to rise

This is the part most easily missed, and worth being precise about.

A pension contract calculates one number at signing - from the premium, your age and a guaranteed interest rate - and pays that number for life. There is no CPI link, no wage link, and no statutory indexation duty on Pillar II at all. That is a genuine asymmetry with Pillar I, which the law indexes every 1 April.

The only route upward is lisakasum, a share of the insurer's technical profit. Compensa's own contract terms state that the insurer "has no obligation to pay additional profit, and the policyholder has no contractual right to demand its payment" (§14.1). A rise worth less than 5% may be handed over as a one-off instead of being raised into the ongoing pension (§14.4), so small allocations never compound. A statutory floor exists on what share of technical profit must eventually be distributed - but none on whether profit exists in any given year.

So the base case for planning is zero increases. At 2.5% inflation a fixed payment loses about a quarter of its buying power in ten years, and close to half in twenty-five. Over a retirement starting at 63 and running to 100, that is not a detail.

The fund pension behaves in the opposite way. It redeems a set number of units at each payment and values them at whatever the fund is worth that day, so the balance keeps compounding while it is drawn down. In an equity fund the payment tends to rise in real terms. But it rides the market with no floor: a bad decade shows up directly in the payment, and it can fall in nominal terms, which a genuine index never would.

The annuity is also the more fragile arrangement. It is

the only payout that is both tax-free and genuinely lifelong, which is why any plan leaning on pension income wants it. But the market has gone from several insurers to one, and two features make that thinner than a single-supplier market usually is:

No insurer is obliged to write one. KindlTS §590 classes the pension contract as ordinary voluntary insurance. There is no duty to quote, and an insurer may refuse a pot too small to be worth administering.

The state backstop covers only existing contracts. When insurers left the market, Sotsiaalkindlustusamet took over servicing the contracts they had already written. It does not write new ones. If the last seller stops, someone retiring after that has nowhere to go.

The 2021 reform, which let anyone leave Pillar II outright, plausibly pushes it further the same way: people expecting a long life annuitise, others take the money, and that selection raises the price for whoever is left. No Estonian study of this since 2021 has been found, so treat it as a mechanism to watch rather than an established finding.

The fund pension needs nobody's permission. It is paid from your own fund at a pace set by statute, and no company has to agree to anything. Its cost is that it runs out: the duration is remaining life expectancy at the age you start, which at 65 is 19.3 years combined — and the years from there to 100 come back to the portfolio, at the oldest and least recoverable point in the plan.

One caveat on that number: Statistics Estonia publishes it by age and sex, and the gap is wide — about 16 years for men against 21 for women at 65. The simulator uses the combined figure, and Pensionikeskus's own calculator applies the life table from the year before last, rounded to whole years. Treat the end date as approximate.

So model it both ways. The simulator will do either, and the difference is the price of not depending on an insurer. If the plan only works with the annuity, it has a single point of failure that no amount of saving fixes — and that is worth knowing now, decades before the contract has to be signed.
