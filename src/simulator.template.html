Ownership of future savings

All income is pooled and all spending is shared — this decides who owns future savings. Existing assets stay where you entered them.

Household

Other income

Income that arrives whether or not anyone goes to work: rent, a pension already in payment, royalties. Enter it net — after tax and after the costs of producing it. For Estonian residential rent that means after the 20% automatic expense deduction and the 22% tax on the rest, so about 82% of the rent, less the real costs of ownership. It reduces what the portfolio has to fund, so it lowers the FI target directly.

Monthly spending

The single most important input, and the one people get wrong. Count everything that leaves the account, not what you think you ought to spend — a year of bank statements divided by twelve beats a budget. Leave out anything already entered elsewhere: Pillar III contributions and insurance premiums are deducted separately and would count twice.

Housing now is rent or your current mortgage plus utilities and upkeep; if you are buying, it is replaced by the new mortgage and running costs from the move onward. Child costs remain in the cash flow until the end year you provide; if blank, the model conservatively assumes no end. Everything else and Buffer are both permanent, and are what the FI number is actually built from.

Circumstances

Children or other dependents turns on the checks that only matter when someone relies on your income — chiefly life and disability cover. It does not change any figure by itself; child costs are entered above.

Exclude crypto keeps crypto out of the target. On by default: a plan resting on it is a bet rather than a plan, and the balance is still shown so the total is visible. Untick it and it counts as spendable, which will move the FI date a long way.

Buying a home

Everything here is entered in today's money, like the rest of the page. Price and Deposit decide the loan and so the LTV, which the Bank of Estonia caps at 85%. Term is capped at 30 years by the same rules; a shorter one costs more per month but far less in total interest. Running costs are what the home costs to hold — utilities, upkeep, the association fee, insurance — and they are permanent, so unlike the mortgage they stay in the FI target forever. Moving & furnishing is a one-off that leaves the portfolio at purchase, alongside about 2% of the price in notary, state and bank fees. Months away decides how much longer the money keeps compounding before it is spent.

How long, and how much

The after-FIRE reserve stays in cash and is never spent on ordinary expenses. Cash above it is spent first, then investments. Investments replenish the reserve if its real value falls. The house-purchase cushion can fund the same reserve; it is not counted twice. Emergency withdrawals are not simulated. Enter 0 to disable protection.

Model update: cash now retains its cash return after FIRE and house completion. Older plans may produce different dates. Market stress affects accessible investments; cash keeps its configured return and pension projections are unchanged in those tests.

The number of months of post-purchase household spending kept available after paying the deposit, transaction costs, and moving or furnishing costs. The simulator adds this cash cushion to the amount required on completion day and does not treat it as available for the purchase. Six months is the default; a less stable income, dependants, or uncertain repair costs may justify more. Setting it to zero assumes no emergency cash remains after completion.

There is no "work until" setting: the age you can stop is what the tool solves for, and it appears in the results as age at FI. To move it, change spending, saving, or what the pension is allowed to contribute.

More on the withdrawal rate , spending growth , how long the money must last , and why inflation appears at all .

The share of the portfolio you take each year, and the number the whole target is divided by. 3.5% rather than the familiar 4%: that figure came from a study of 30-year retirements, and a plan starting in your forties runs closer to 50. Longer horizons fail more often at the same rate, so a prudent planning rate generally falls as the horizon grows. Every 0.5% you add cuts the target substantially - which is exactly why it deserves suspicion rather than optimism.

Whether the same life gets more expensive faster than inflation. Zero - the usual FIRE assumption - says today's spending stays constant in real terms through the modeled horizon, and is probably a little optimistic: care and health costs outrun the general index, and lifestyles drift. 0.5-1% is a reasonable stress test. It compounds hard over a long retirement, and if it ever reaches the withdrawal rate no finite pot survives it, which the tool will say rather than returning a large number. This setting does not increase contractual mortgage payments. Mortgage inflation erosion and interest-rate repricing are not yet modeled: the payment currently stays flat in the real-euro schedule.

How long the money has to last. In a two-person household it is the younger person who has to reach it, so the older one will be past it - the money has to outlive whoever lives longest. It only bites when the pension is counted: a plan that maintains an SWR capital floor is still tested over this horizon. Estonian remaining life expectancy at 65 is about 19 years, so an average pensioner reaches 84 - but the horizon has to cover the tail, not the average, and for a couple the chance one of them passes 95 is not small. Raising it costs surprisingly little, because money needed that far out is discounted by decades of compounding.

Needed for exactly one thing: a pension annuity is a fixed nominal amount for life, with no CPI or wage link and no indexation duty on Pillar II, so its buying power falls every year. Nothing else on this page needs an inflation figure, because the return is already real. 2.5% rather than the ECB's 2% target, since Estonian inflation has run above the euro-area average.

What the portfolio has to do

Letting the pension take over

Ignoring pensions means building the selected portfolio target without relying on pension income. The withdrawal-rate target is a planning heuristic, not a guarantee. Contributions stop on the day you stop working, so retiring earlier buys a smaller pension; that is priced in. More on the three settings, on when and how the pots are taken, and on counting only part of them.

On the state pension specifically. Sotsiaalkindlustusamet's own calculator returns roughly 70–80% of our Pillar I figure for someone retiring in the 2050s. Every mechanism agrees — its Pillar II reduction matches ours to three decimals — but its absolute level is lower, and that tool is deliberately conservative: its Pillar II projection assumes no investment growth at all, which its own page states. Treat it as a floor rather than a rival forecast. Believing the state pension at 70–80% is a defensible way to sit between the two, and checking your own case there is better than either number.

Note what the first option also changes. Pension counting and the portfolio-end choice are independent. The capital-floor option preserves the selected SWR floor through the modeled horizon; drawdown permits the balance to reach zero at the planning age. Neither is a guarantee.

Counting a pension at less than 100% is how you build a margin against it disappointing — and the margin lives in the portfolio, because a smaller assumed pension forces a bigger pot. If the pension then arrives in full, the surplus is yours; if it does not, the plan still works. That is the whole trade, and it is bought with working years.

The two are separate because they fail differently. Pillars II and III are a balance you own: what is uncertain is its future value — returns, fees, what an annuity costs to buy, whether one can be bought at all. Pillar I is a promise, and what is uncertain is whether it is kept on today's terms. A common shape is to believe your own pots in full and haircut the state pension; American planners routinely count 70–75% of Social Security for the same reason.

Pillars II and III are your own money, in an account in your name, in funds you chose, and since 2021 the whole balance can be withdrawn early at 22%. Pillar I is a promise, funded by whoever is working at the time and indexed 80% to social tax receipts - a government can change it without touching anyone's balance, which is why it is off by default. Turn it on to see what it would add, not to depend on it.

Start drawing decides whether the pots are taken at the first legal opportunity (state pension age -5) or left invested until the state pension arrives. Deferring compounds the pot for five more years and starts everything together, at the cost of a longer stretch on the portfolio alone.

Take as is the choice between a lifetime annuity - paid until you die, but a fixed euro amount that inflation erodes, sold by one insurer - and a fund pension, which rides the fund and needs no insurer, but runs out after about 19 years.
